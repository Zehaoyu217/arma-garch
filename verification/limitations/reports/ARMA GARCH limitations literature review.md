# Published theory explains most sarimax_garch findings

The econometrics and software literature already explains four of the study's seven findings, supports a fifth as a genuine extension, and is silent on two software checks. **E3 is a known result**: Newey and Steigerwald (1997) proved that a non-Gaussian quasi-likelihood identifies the mean only under symmetry, while the Gaussian QMLE needs only the first two conditional moments right (Bollerslev and Wooldridge 1992). **E1, E2 and E6 rest on documented mechanisms**: R's own `arima` help says AIC is valid only for exact-likelihood fits, E2's default-band coverages are the standardized-t(5) quantiles to the third decimal, and each piece of E6 has an anchor in weak-ARMA theory, Christoffersen's conditional coverage, the Demos-Sentana and Andrews boundary laws and Ma-Nelson-Startz's weak identification. **E4 is an extension**: Engle's (1982) block-diagonality theorem and Sin (2003) allow a misspecified variance to bias jointly estimated means, but no paper gives the sign or size of the effect when symmetric GARCH is fitted to GJR data. **E5 and E7 are package-specific checks**; arch's `MAMean`, which E7 matches within 0.008 SE, was released on 2026-11-11 and maximises the same conditional likelihood as the package. Three derivations in this review sharpen the findings: the E1 gap is the log-density of the unscored observations, so its sign flips with the units of y; statsmodels' default `opg` covariance is the most anti-conservative textbook choice under GARCH, which explains E6's AR/MA under-coverage; and the t(5) design behind E2 and E5 has an infinite fourth moment, outside Francq and Zakoian's (2004) normality conditions. In practice the Gaussian default is the theoretically safe estimator of the mean, the t and symmetric-GARCH options carry known mean-bias risks that SARIMAX does not, several printed diagnostics have the wrong null, and arch 9.0 has narrowed the package's niche to joint ARMA(p,q)-GARCH. These verdicts rest mostly on abstracts and secondary descriptions rather than full texts, and no published Monte Carlo comparator turned up for any of the study's magnitudes.

## Four findings restate theory, one extends it, two are software checks

Split each finding into mechanism and magnitude and the verdicts sort themselves. The mechanisms are mostly decades old. The magnitudes are new, because none of the sources simulates regression-with-ARMA-GARCH at the study's sample sizes and parameter values. Study figures throughout come from the study's own record (`verification/limitations/README.md`). The table gives each verdict and its anchors; the sections that follow argue them.

## Gaussian QMLE needs a correct variance and four moments, not normality

The package's Gaussian default rests on **Bollerslev and Wooldridge (1992)**. When the conditional mean and the conditional variance are both correctly specified, the normal score stays a martingale difference. The joint Gaussian QMLE is then consistent and asymptotically normal whatever the innovation distribution, with the sandwich A⁻¹BA⁻¹ as its robust covariance ([Bollerslev & Wooldridge 1992](https://doi.org/10.1080/07474939208800229)). A 2025 restatement adds the qualifier that matters in practice: the result holds subject to the existence of enough finite moments ([Wang, Wooldridge & Xu 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/jtsa.12770)). For ARMA-GARCH, **Francq and Zakoian (2004)** prove strong consistency under strict stationarity and a small fractional moment. Their condition for asymptotic normality is a finite fourth moment of the standardized innovation η for pure GARCH, but a **finite fourth moment of the observed process** once an ARMA mean is added ([Francq & Zakoian 2004](https://doi.org/10.3150/bj/1093265632)). That weakens the higher-moment conditions of [Ling and McAleer (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0266466603192092). Ling's (2007) self-weighted QMLE drops the process-moment requirement altogether by discounting large observations ([Ling 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2006.07.016)). Lee and Hansen (1994) and Lumsdaine (1996), who also covers the integrated case, give the pure GARCH(1,1) results ([Lee & Hansen 1994](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0266466600008215); [Lumsdaine 1996](https://doi.org/10.2307/2171862)). Weiss (1986) is the ARCH-only precursor ([Weiss 1986](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0266466600011397)).

The docstring's "E[ε⁴] < ∞" is therefore correct for ARMA-GARCH only if ε is the observed series and η also has four moments, and it is sufficient, not necessary. These conditions come from secondary descriptions; the theorem texts were not read.

The condition matters for the study's own designs. A GARCH(1,1) series has a finite fourth moment when β² + 2αβ + κα² < 1, where κ is the kurtosis of η (the standard condition, evaluated here):

| Design | Shocks | κ | β² + 2αβ + κα² | Fourth moment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Persistent (α 0.08, β 0.90) | normal | 3 | 0.973 | finite |
| Same α, β (E2 and E5) | t(5) | 9 | **1.012** | infinite |
| E3, λ −0.5 (Hansen skew-t, ν 6) | skewed t | 9.1 | **1.012** | infinite |
| E3, milder designs | skewed t | 6.0 to 7.0 | 0.992 to 0.999 | finite, just |

The two flagged cells lie outside the Francq-Zakoian normality conditions for the Gaussian QMLE of the mean. In E3, the Gaussian fit's robust intercept coverage is lowest (0.92) in exactly the cell where the condition fails.

The same check one level up is E(β + αη²)⁴ < 1, for an eighth moment. It fails for the persistent design even with normal shocks (1.015). So a sandwich built from y²ε² products has infinite variance there. Its sampling distribution is right-skewed and falls below its target in most samples. That may be why SARIMAX's robust standard errors in E6 still cover only 0.89-0.94.

Joint estimation earns its keep through efficiency. **Engle's (1982) Theorem 4** shows that the information matrix between regression and ARCH parameters is block-diagonal when the model is "symmetric and regular": the variance is an even function of past errors and the density is symmetric. The two blocks can then be estimated separately with no asymptotic loss ([Engle 1982](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1912773)). A secondary account says Bollerslev (1986) carries the property over to GARCH ([Bollerslev 1986](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-4076%2886%2990063-1)). Engle's abstract also reports that least squares keeps its usual optimality properties, while maximum likelihood is more efficient, by a factor that can be unbounded. SARIMAX is the least-squares-type estimator here: consistent but inefficient.

E6's gains from joint estimation follow Engle's direction. With persistent GARCH, slope RMSE falls 3-17% and AR/MA coefficient RMSE 4-16%; with typical monthly GARCH, slope RMSE falls 0-7%. No source gives an efficiency figure at comparable parameter values, so the sizes are the study's own.

Block-diagonality also bounds the cost of the two-step route that statsforecast's documentation recommends: fit ARMA, then fit GARCH to its residuals ([statsforecast docs](https://nixtlaverse.nixtla.io/statsforecast/docs/models/garch.html.md)). With a zero cross block, first-step error does not leak into the variance estimates, but the first-step mean stays inefficient. Under leverage or skewed shocks the cross block no longer vanishes.

Ignoring GARCH leaves SARIMAX's point estimates intact and its standard errors wrong. Weak-ARMA theory covers ARMA estimation when the noise is uncorrelated but not independent. Least squares stays consistent and asymptotically normal, but its covariance differs from the iid formula ([Francq & Zakoian 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0378-3758%2897%2900139-0)). Bartlett's formula for autocorrelations fails without iid noise ([Romano & Thombs 1996](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1996.10476928)), and the portmanteau tests need correcting ([Francq, Roy & Zakoian 2005](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214504000001510)).

The abstracts do not say which way the error goes, but a short argument does (a derivation, not a cited result). The score for an AR coefficient is yₜ₋₁εₜ, and under GARCH its variance E[y²ₜ₋₁hₜ] exceeds σ²E[y²ₜ₋₁], because a large lagged shock raises both y²ₜ₋₁ and the conditional variance hₜ. The outer product B therefore exceeds the Hessian A, which gives B⁻¹ < A⁻¹ < A⁻¹BA⁻¹: **the outer-product-of-gradients estimator, statsmodels' default `cov_type='opg'`, is the most anti-conservative of the three textbook choices**, the Hessian is less so, and only the sandwich (`cov_type='robust'`) is consistent ([statsmodels MLEModel.fit](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/generated/statsmodels.tsa.statespace.mlemodel.MLEModel.fit.html)).

E6 reproduces that ordering. opg intervals cover the AR/MA coefficients 0.83-0.92, SARIMAX's robust ones 0.89-0.94, and the joint GARCH fit's robust ones 0.91-0.97. For a regressor independent of the volatility process, E[x²h] = E[x²]σ², so B = A and all three choices agree. That is why slope and intercept intervals cover 0.91-0.97 under every choice. The direction and sizes extend known theory, and the README's advice to refit SARIMAX with `cov_type="robust"` is what the theory prescribes.

None of this protects the mean when the variance model itself is wrong. Bollerslev and Wooldridge assume a correct variance. A sandwich fixes the spread of an estimator, not its centre, so under a misspecified variance it covers the pseudo-true value rather than the truth. That explains E4's robust intercept coverage of 0.745. No source found studies sandwich coverage under variance misspecification directly.

## A symmetric-t likelihood estimates the centre of skewed shocks, not their mean

E3 restates a known identification result. **Newey and Steigerwald (1997)** showed that a non-Gaussian quasi-likelihood in a conditional-heteroskedasticity model peaks at the true mean and scale only in two cases: the conditional mean is identically zero, or a symmetry condition holds (true and assumed densities symmetric and unimodal). Otherwise the innovation density needs its own location parameter, which costs the GARCH variance parameters no efficiency ([Newey & Steigerwald 1997](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2171754)). Wang, Wooldridge and Xu (2025) state the practical consequence. t-likelihood mean and variance estimators are generally inconsistent when the t is misspecified, asymmetry included, while the Gaussian QMLE needs only the first two moments right ([Wang, Wooldridge & Xu 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/jtsa.12770)).

The mechanism is Huber's: an M-estimator that minimises a non-quadratic loss converges to a functional other than the mean when the distribution is asymmetric ([Huber 1964](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177703732)). The study's own explanation is that mechanism written out. The t score weights each shock by (ν+1)/(ν−2+z²), so the long left tail is down-weighted. The weighted mean of ε is then zero only when the fitted centre sits above the true mean.

The magnitudes pass a sanity check. For Hansen's skew-t with ν 6 and λ −0.5, arch's constants give a = −0.75 and b = 1.09. The mode therefore lies **+0.69 SD** above the mean (computed here). The study's pseudo-true t centre of **+0.122 SD** sits between mean and mode, where M-estimation theory puts it. A constant location shift loads on the intercept alone. The slope, AR coefficient and GARCH parameters therefore stay essentially unbiased, while the intercept moves +1.2 to +4.9 SD and its 95% coverage falls to 0.79, 0.26 and 0.00 at T = 2000. A search for a published simulation with a skewed truth and a t pseudo-density found none, so the sizes are new even though the mechanism is not.

Location is not the only parameter at risk. **Fan, Qi and Xiu (2014)** show that a non-Gaussian QMLE is inconsistent unless its family contains the true density. An unknown scale factor must equal one for the volatility scale to be recovered, and estimating a shape parameter does not cure this. Their three-step estimator restores consistency and beats the Gaussian QMLE under heavy tails ([Fan, Qi & Xiu 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350015.2013.840239); [working paper](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1001.3895)). Francq, Lepage and Zakoian (2011) give a two-stage non-Gaussian QMLE and a test of whether the Gaussian QMLE is already efficient ([Francq, Lepage & Zakoian 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2011.08.001)). **Fiorentini and Sentana (2019)** characterise which mean and variance parameters a distributionally misspecified ML estimator still estimates consistently. They give closed-form consistent estimators for the rest, covering regressions and GARCH-in-mean ([Fiorentini & Sentana 2019](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:econom:v:213:y:2019:i:2:p:321-358)). Engle and Gonzalez-Rivera's semiparametric ARCH avoids choosing a density at all ([Engle & Gonzalez-Rivera 1991](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bes:jnlbes:v:9:y:1991:i:4:p:345-59)).

E3's GARCH parameters escaped the scale problem in its design, so its intercept bias is the pure Newey-Steigerwald location effect. The package's `dist="t"` is the unadjusted one-stage estimator, and the fixes, in rising order of effort, are an intercept check against the Gaussian fit (the README's one-SE rule), a two- or three-step scheme anchored on the Gaussian QMLE, or a density that nests the truth.

The nesting route needs care, because the skewed families are not interchangeable. **Hansen's (1994)** skew-t has tail parameter η and skewness λ ∈ (−1, 1), symmetric at λ = 0, and is arch's `SkewStudent` ([Hansen 1994](https://doi.org/10.2307/2527081); [arch SkewStudent](https://bashtage.github.io/arch/univariate/generated/arch.univariate.SkewStudent.html)). **Fernández and Steel (1998)** skew any symmetric unimodal density with inverse scale factors on the two half-lines, with ξ > 0 and symmetry at ξ = 1; rugarch's `sstd`, `snorm` and `sged` and fGarch's `sstd` use it ([Fernández & Steel 1998](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1998.10474117); [rugarch vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/rugarch/vignettes/Introduction_to_the_rugarch_package.pdf); [fGarch docs](https://rdrr.io/cran/fGarch/man/dist-sstd.html)). Jones and Faddy's two-shape skew-t ([Jones & Faddy 2003](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9868.00378)), Theodossiou's skewed generalized t ([Theodossiou 1998](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.44.12.1650)), rugarch's Johnson SU, and Harvey and Siddique's non-central t with time-varying skewness, whose inclusion changes the estimated variance persistence ([Harvey & Siddique 1999](https://doi.org/10.2307/2676230)), are further distinct constructions, and Bauwens and Laurent carry the Fernández-Steel idea to multivariate GARCH ([Bauwens & Laurent 2005](https://doi.org/10.1198/073500104000000523)).

The same nominal skewness implies different shapes and mode locations across families, so E3's skewness values belong to Hansen's λ. Empirically, APARCH with skewed-Student innovations gave better daily VaR for long and short positions than normal or symmetric-t versions ([Giot & Laurent 2003](https://doi.org/10.1002/jae.710)). The sources contain no verified figures for typical standardized-residual skewness.

## Symmetric GARCH turns the intercept into a leverage proxy

The asymmetric models are well documented. **GJR (1993)** adds an indicator term so that negative shocks raise next-period variance more than positive ones ([Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1993.tb05128.x)); **EGARCH** models log variance with sign and magnitude terms and needs no positivity constraints ([Nelson 1991](https://doi.org/10.2307/2938260)); Zakoian's **TGARCH** is a threshold model for the conditional standard deviation, so the label is inexact for GJR's variance form ([Zakoian 1994](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-1889%2894%2990039-6)); and **APARCH** nests GARCH, GJR and TGARCH through an estimated power ([Ding, Granger & Engle 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0927-5398%2893%2990006-D)).

arch fits GJR as `GARCH(p=1, o=1, q=1)` and TARCH with `power=1.0`, alongside EGARCH and APARCH ([arch GARCH docs](https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/univariate/generated/arch.univariate.GARCH.html)). rugarch offers `gjrGARCH`, `eGARCH`, `apARCH` and the `fGARCH` family ([rugarch manual](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/rugarch/rugarch.pdf)). The package has none of them. GJR is the cheapest to add because it keeps the variance recursion linear in its parameters. Under symmetric shocks GJR persistence is α + γ/2 + β, and any "persistence 0.97" should say it uses that convention.

**Engle and Ng (1993)** introduced the news impact curve and three tests (sign bias, negative size bias and positive size bias), plus a joint test. On daily Japanese stock returns they found GJR the best parametric model; EGARCH captured most of the asymmetry but implied too variable a variance ([Engle & Ng 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1993.tb05127.x)). The regression form usually implemented regresses squared standardized residuals on a negative-shock dummy and its interactions. That form comes from software documentation; the paper's own equations were not read. Lundbergh and Teräsvirta (2002) put asymmetry into a family of LM tests for an estimated GARCH. Their nulls allow for estimation, and robust versions handle non-normal errors ([Lundbergh & Teräsvirta 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-4076%2802%2900096-9)).

Whether any of this matters depends on the asset. In a comparison of 330 volatility models, GARCH(1,1) was clearly worse than leverage models for IBM returns, while nothing beat it for DM-USD ([Hansen & Lunde 2005](https://doi.org/10.1002/jae.800)). A symmetric default is defensible for exchange rates and risky for equity-like series. E4's equity-typical design (α 0, leverage 0.14) produced the largest bias. Evidence for macro, interest-rate and credit series could not be verified, and their sign conventions differ: a "negative shock" may be unexpectedly low inflation.

Theory says a misspecified variance can move the mean; it does not say which way. Engle's block-diagonality needs a variance that is even in past errors. GJR's is not, so the cross block between intercept and variance parameters need not vanish ([Engle 1982](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1912773)). **Sin (2003)** addresses the textbook result that linear-exponential-family QML mean estimates stay consistent under misspecified higher moments. That result fails when the weighting matrix and the mean parameters are functionally related, and Sin names ARMA-GARCH as the prominent example ([Sin 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0731-9053%2803%2917009-0)). In a GARCH likelihood the variance is built from εₜ₋₁ = yₜ₋₁ − mean, so the link is there. Gouriéroux, Monfort and Trognon's mean-only pseudo-likelihood theory is the other half: when the variance does not depend on the mean parameters, the mean estimate stays consistent whatever the variance ([Gouriéroux, Monfort & Trognon 1984](https://doi.org/10.2307/1913471)). That is why SARIMAX's intercept stays unbiased in E4. GJR themselves give the nearest empirical precedent, in a GARCH-in-mean setting: correcting the variance specification turned the estimated risk-return relation from positive or null to negative ([GJR 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1993.tb05128.x)). No paper simulates the study's case, so E4 is an extension.

The study's score argument also gives an intuition the literature does not state. The Gaussian score for the intercept carries the term ½(ε²ₜ/σ²ₜ − 1)σₜ⁻²·∂σ²ₜ/∂c, with ∂σ²ₜ/∂c = −2α·Σβʲεₜ₋₁₋ⱼ. Under GJR, the symmetric fit's variance is too low after negative shocks, where ∂σ²ₜ/∂c > 0. It is too high after positive shocks, where ∂σ²ₜ/∂c < 0. So the product has a positive mean, and the intercept rises until the residual term offsets it.

Read geometrically (a derivation, not a published result), raising c by δ shifts every past residual down by δ. The symmetric news-impact parabola α(ε − δ)² then responds more to negative returns than to positive ones. **The symmetric model creates the missing asymmetry by moving the centre of its news-impact curve.** Asymmetric variants such as rugarch's NAGARCH build in the same shifted parabola on purpose.

The cost is +0.6 SD of intercept bias at leverage 0.06, +1.4 SD at 0.14, and robust coverage of 0.745, far worse than anything SARIMAX suffers. With leverage in the data, the richer symmetric model is worse for the mean than no GARCH at all. A GJR term or an Engle-Ng check therefore belongs before any mean inference on equity-like series. No source examines what ignoring leverage does to prediction-interval coverage.

## Forecast bands fail three separate ways: shape, parameter error and timing

**Shape.** **Baillie and Bollerslev (1992)** derived the minimum-MSE predictor and its conditional MSE for a dynamic model with ARMA disturbances and GARCH innovations. They also gave the moments of the GARCH(1,1) prediction-error distribution and Cornish-Fisher prediction intervals ([Baillie & Bollerslev 1992](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-4076%2892%2990066-Z)). The correction is needed because a multi-step GARCH forecast error is a variance mixture of normals, fat-tailed even under Gaussian shocks. That is why the normal band in E2's normal-shock design drifts to 0.810 at 80% and 0.983 at 99% by h = 20, with excess kurtosis 0.8.

With t(5) shocks the one-step mismatch is pure arithmetic. Scaling standardized t(5) by √(3/5), a normal-shaped band covers **0.841 at 80%, 0.913 at 90%, 0.947 at 95% and 0.979 at 99%** (computed here). Those are E2's 0.84 and 0.978 to the third decimal. The README's "right by coincidence at 95%" is the point where the t(5) and normal quantiles cross. No paper states the crossover, because none needs to. Simulating with the fitted density, as `method="simulated"` does, removes the shape error. A Cornish-Fisher band is the analytic alternative the package lacks.

**Parameter error.** Plug-in bands ignore estimation error, and the cost has been documented for three decades. Chatfield (1993) explains why prediction intervals tend to be too narrow in practice and separates formulas with and without a parameter-uncertainty correction ([Chatfield 1993](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350015.1993.10509938)); Thombs and Schucany (1990) bootstrap autoregressive intervals conditional on the last observations ([Thombs & Schucany 1990](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1990.10476225)); and **Kim (2001)** finds asymptotic and standard bootstrap intervals often too narrow, most of all with unit or near-unit roots, and proposes bootstrap-after-bootstrap intervals that are accurate and conservative ([Kim 2001](https://doi.org/10.1198/07350010152472670)). For GARCH, **Pascual, Romo and Ruiz** re-estimate the parameters in every bootstrap replicate so that estimation variability enters the interval, first for ARIMA without a backward representation, which extends the method to MA terms ([Pascual, Romo & Ruiz 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9892.2004.01713.x)), then for GARCH returns and volatilities without a distributional assumption ([Pascual, Romo & Ruiz 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csda.2004.12.008)).

The software differs in how much of this it implements. rugarch's `ugarchboot` implements the 2006 method as `"full"`, which is slow because it refits. Its `"partial"` method resamples innovations only and gives no interval for the one-step σ ([rugarch ugarchboot](https://rdrr.io/cran/rugarch/man/ugarchboot-methods.html)). arch's `"bootstrap"` forecast draws standardized residuals with the fitted parameters held fixed ([arch forecasting docs](https://arch.readthedocs.io/en/latest/univariate/forecasting.html)). Christoffersen and Gonçalves find estimation risk in VaR and expected-shortfall forecasts substantial even for tightly parameterised dynamic variance models ([Christoffersen & Gonçalves 2005](https://www.risk.net/journal-risk/2161126/estimation-risk-financial-risk-management)).

E2's D4 cell, where a simulated 90% band covers 0.861 at h = 20 when T = 250 and φ = 0.9, is Kim's near-unit-root narrowness measured for ARMA-GARCH. The package's simulated band is plug-in, not a Pascual-Romo-Ruiz bootstrap, and the documentation should not suggest otherwise.

Filtered historical simulation is a middle route. **Barone-Adesi, Giannopoulos and Vosper (1999)** filter returns through GARCH, resample the standardized residuals and simulate forward ([Barone-Adesi, Giannopoulos & Vosper 1999](https://doi.org/10.1002/%28SICI%291096-9934%28199908%2919:5%3C583::AID-FUT5%3E3.0.CO;2-S)). This refines Hull and White's volatility-updated historical simulation ([Hull & White 1998](https://doi.org/10.21314/jor.1998.001)). The shocks become empirical while the dynamics stay parametric, so FHS fixes tail shape but not parameter error. E3's FHS band brings the 1% tails back to 1.06-1.08% under skew at T = 2000, but it would not fix D4's long-horizon shortfall. arch's bootstrap forecast and rugarch's `"partial"` method are FHS in all but name (an inference from their documentation).

**Timing.** Kupiec's (1995) proportion-of-failures test checks only the average hit rate ([Kupiec 1995](https://doi.org/10.3905/jod.1995.407942)). **Christoffersen (1998)** observed that interval evaluation had implicitly assumed homoskedastic errors and tested only unconditional coverage. He added likelihood-ratio tests of independence and of conditional coverage ([Christoffersen 1998](https://doi.org/10.2307/2527341)). A constant-width band can be right on average and wrong exactly when it matters. That is E6's headline: SARIMAX's 90% band averages 0.88-0.90, but covers 0.91-0.96 in the calmest third of periods and 0.81-0.86 in the most turbulent third. The GARCH band holds 0.86-0.90 in both. Splitting on the true σ² for period T+1 is a sharper, simulation-only cousin of Christoffersen's test. No published Monte Carlo of calm-versus-turbulent coverage turned up, so the split is an extension.

Coverage alone cannot rank a wide calibrated band against a sharp one. The probability integral transform ([Diebold, Gunther & Tay 1998](https://doi.org/10.2307/2527342)) and proper scoring rules such as the CRPS and the interval score ([Gneiting & Raftery 2007](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)) complete the picture, read as sharpness subject to calibration ([Gneiting, Balabdaoui & Raftery 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9868.2007.00587.x)). E6 shows why scores and coverage must be read together: its CRPS ratios stay within 0.984-1.003 while conditional coverage shifts by ten points.

## Textbook residual tests lose their null distributions after a GARCH fit

The m − p − q degrees-of-freedom rule is an iid-ARMA result. **Box and Pierce (1970)** derive χ²(m − p − q) for the residual portmanteau of a correctly specified ARMA with iid innovations ([Box & Pierce 1970](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1970.10481180)). Ljung and Box (1978) improve its finite-sample fit under the same conditions ([Ljung & Box 1978](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/65.2.297)). McLeod and Li (1983) apply the portmanteau to squared ARMA residuals to check for ARCH-type nonlinearity ([McLeod & Li 1983](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9892.1983.tb00373.x)). That is valid on SARIMAX residuals before any GARCH is fitted.

After a GARCH fit, both statistics follow a different distribution. **Li and Mak (1994)** derive the asymptotic distribution of squared standardized-residual autocorrelations allowing for the estimated variance parameters ([Li & Mak 1994](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9892.1994.tb00217.x)); Wong and Ling (2005) give the joint distribution of level and squared autocorrelations and a mixed portmanteau ([Wong & Ling 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9892.2005.00420.x)); and Carbon and Francq (2011) show that for asymmetric power GARCH the squared-residual vector has a non-identity covariance, so the portmanteau is a weighted rather than a plain χ² ([Carbon & Francq 2011](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/27686/)). For SARIMAX residuals under GARCH errors, Francq, Roy and Zakoian (2005) show that Box-Pierce and Ljung-Box can perform poorly, and supply a corrected test ([Francq, Roy & Zakoian 2005](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214504000001510)).

The README's fix of passing `model_df = p + q` to `acorr_ljungbox` is the iid-ARMA convention. It is better than no adjustment, but it is not a valid correction for zₜ. On z²ₜ, the parameters that matter are GARCH's, not the ARMA order.

rugarch does not fully solve the problem either. Its code imports **Fisher and Gallagher's (2012)** weighted portmanteau statistics, which down-weight longer lags and use a gamma approximation ([Fisher & Gallagher 2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2012.688465)). It uses them for two different statistics: a weighted Ljung-Box on standardized and squared standardized residuals, and a "weighted Li-Mak" ARCH-LM. Both are run at lags 1, 3·df − 1 and 5·df − 1 ([rugarch tests source](https://rdrr.io/cran/rugarch/src/R/rugarch-tests.R)). The same code computes the Engle-Ng joint test as an unadjusted linear-hypothesis χ². Its adjusted Pearson goodness-of-fit uses χ²(g − 1) with no deduction for estimated parameters, so those printed p-values are approximate too.

The package's ARCH-LM on zₜ with χ²(lags) inherits Engle's null, which assumed no ARCH had been estimated ([Engle 1982](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1912773)). Lundbergh and Teräsvirta's LM tests of no remaining ARCH, parameter constancy and asymmetry have standard nulls after estimation, and their robust versions do better under non-normal errors ([Lundbergh & Teräsvirta 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-4076%2802%2900096-9)). No source says which way the naive test's size is distorted.

Moment-based checks fare worse under fat tails. The textbook skewness test, with variance 6/T, needs iid normality. **Bai and Ng (2005)** replace those variances with long-run (HAC) ones. They find symmetry tests well behaved but kurtosis tests badly distorted except for thin-tailed data ([Bai & Ng 2005](https://doi.org/10.1198/073500104000000271)). Premaratne (2005) builds a symmetry test for leptokurtic returns ([Premaratne 2005](https://doi.org/10.1093/jjfinec/nbi009)).

E3 found that under t(6) the residual skewness has SD 0.23 at T = 2000, four times √(6/T) = 0.055. That is the theory at work: the variance of the sample skewness involves E[z⁶], which is infinite for t(ν) with ν ≤ 6, so the statistic converges more slowly than √T. Jarque-Bera tests a null of iid normality ([Jarque & Bera 1980](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-1765%2880%2990024-5); [Jarque & Bera 1987](https://doi.org/10.2307/1403192)). Printing it beside a t fit tests something the fit already denies.

The honest check of a fitted density is the probability integral transform (PIT). It can be tested for uniformity and independence ([Diebold, Gunther & Tay 1998](https://doi.org/10.2307/2527342)), or with Berkowitz's likelihood-ratio test on inverse-normal PITs, which has good power at T as small as 100 ([Berkowitz 2001](https://doi.org/10.1198/07350010152596718)). The caveat: in-sample PITs built from estimated parameters make the standard critical values inexact.

## Likelihoods compare only on the same observations under one definition

### Toggling `garch` changes the likelihood, the sample and the model at once

Conditional and exact Gaussian ARMA likelihoods are different objective functions. They are asymptotically equivalent for stationary, invertible models, but **Ansley and Newbold (1980)** found that asymptotically equivalent ARMA estimators (exact ML, least squares, conditional least squares) can differ substantially in moderate samples ([Ansley & Newbold 1980](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-4076%2880%2990013-5)). The exact version comes from the Kalman filter's prediction-error decomposition ([Harvey 1989](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781107049994); [Hamilton 1994](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/77442)).

R's documentation states the comparability rule plainly. `arima`'s AIC is "only valid for method = ML fits". The CSS objective sets earlier innovations to zero and is "mainly for expositional purposes". The `n.cond` argument exists "to allow comparability between different fits" ([R stats::arima](https://stat.ethz.ch/R-manual/R-devel/library/stats/html/arima.html)). Hyndman and Athanasopoulos make the same point for models fitted to differently differenced data ([FPP3 §9.5](https://otexts.com/fpp3/arima-r.html)). statsmodels' own models even differ in which constants their AICs include ([statsmodels issue 6577](https://github.com/statsmodels/statsmodels/issues/6577)). In statsmodels SARIMAX with d = 0, all T observations are scored by the exact stationary-initialised likelihood. With d > 0 the diffuse start-up terms are dropped, and `simple_differencing=True` changes the sample itself ([SARIMAX source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/statsmodels/statsmodels/main/statsmodels/tsa/statespace/sarimax.py); [SARIMAX docs](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/generated/statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax.SARIMAX.html)). E1's rule is known; its sizes are the package's.

The study already identifies the gap as the log-density of the p unscored observations. Two consequences follow that it does not draw (both derived here).

**First, the gap nearly cancels AIC's penalty.** The +1.5 is three-quarters of the two log-likelihood units AIC charges for GARCH's extra α and β. So toggling `garch` lets GARCH in whenever its within-likelihood LR exceeds 1 rather than 4. The null distribution of the LR lies above the one-restriction 50:50 χ²(0)/χ²(1) mixture and, on the study's evidence, below χ²(2). Under those two bounds, P(LR > 1) is 0.16 and 0.61, which brackets the observed 45-55%. For MA(1) the conditional likelihood still scores all T observations, setting ε₀ = 0. The gap is then only a small, scale-free start-up term, and the cross-backend rate (8-12%) sits on the within-engine one.

**Second, the AR gap depends on the units of y.** Each dropped observation contributes ½ln(2πσ²/(1−φ²)) + ½:

| Series | Gap per dropped observation | Effect of toggling `garch` |
|---|---|---|
| Study's unit-variance AR(1), φ 0.5 | +1.56 | AIC biased toward GARCH |
| Same series in decimal returns (σ ≈ 0.01) | −3.0 | AIC biased against GARCH |

No fixed offset can repair the comparison. The only fix is to rescore both models on the same T − p observations in one engine: the study's `fixed α = β = 0` null, or R's `n.cond` logic.

### arch 9.0's MAMean validates the code, not the definition

E6's MA-near-unit-root result shows the same mechanism from another side. At θ = 0.9 with no GARCH, the engine's LR picks GARCH 11% of the time and AIC 21%, while ARCH-LM on SARIMAX residuals stays at 4-6%. The zero-pre-sample start-up transient decays like 0.9ᵗ and looks like a variance burst. arch's developers flag the same limit in the pull request that added `MAMean`. They note the zero-pre-sample recursion matches the exact Kalman filter only after the initial effect decays, and that explosive residuals for larger q are capped rather than prevented ([arch PR 903](https://github.com/bashtage/arch/pull/903)).

That is why E7 should be read precisely. **arch 9.0.0, released on 2026-11-11, added `MAMean`** ([arch releases](https://github.com/bashtage/arch/releases); [arch issue 147](https://github.com/bashtage/arch/issues/147)). It is a pure MA(q) mean that works with any volatility process and distribution. It is estimated by the conditional likelihood with pre-sample innovations set to zero, whose effect, the docstring says, "vanishes when the process is invertible" ([arch mean.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bashtage/arch/main/arch/univariate/mean.py)). Invertibility is enforced only for q = 1 and 2, and AR terms cannot be combined with it.

The package and arch maximise the same objective. Agreement within 0.008 SE (largest absolute difference 3.7e-4) is what two correct implementations should produce. The log-likelihoods differ by 0.003-0.012 only because the two start the variance recursion differently. E7 validates the implementation, not the likelihood definition, and it is package-specific; no third-party validation of `MAMean` appears in the sources.

E5 has even less literature behind it. A benchmark literature exists because GARCH estimates differ across software, from [Fiorentini, Calzolari and Panattoni (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1002/%28SICI%291099-1255%28199607%2911:4%3C399::AID-JAE401%3E3.0.CO;2-R) on analytic derivatives and computation, through [McCullough and Renfro (1999)](https://doi.org/10.3233/JEM-1999-0160) on benchmarks and software standards, to [Brooks, Burke and Persand (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0169-2070%2800%2900070-4) on estimation accuracy, but their contents were not read, and none of the sources reports how often an optimiser stops at a local optimum. arch's maintainer remarked in 2017 that reliable estimators for full ARFIMA models with ARCH errors are hard to build ([arch issue 147](https://github.com/bashtage/arch/issues/147)). The study found that 9 of 400 t-fits end more than 0.1 log-likelihood units below the best of nine starts, and that the `converged` flag misses most of them. That finding is package-specific.

Two strands of the literature point to remedies. Weak identification of β when α is small flattens the likelihood ([Ma, Nelson & Startz 2007](https://doi.org/10.2202/1558-3708.1434)). The two- and three-step non-Gaussian QMLEs start from the Gaussian fit ([Fan, Qi & Xiu 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350015.2013.840239)), which makes the Gaussian estimate a natural first start for any t-fit.

## The LM test is the clean GARCH pretest; LR and AIC sit on a boundary

**The LM test needs no GARCH fit.** Testing for GARCH before fitting it is the best-understood part of the problem. Engle's LM test is T·R² from regressing squared residuals on their lags, distributed χ²(q) under homoskedasticity ([Engle 1982](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1912773)). **Lee (1991)** showed the LM test against GARCH disturbances is the same statistic as the LM test against ARCH ([Lee 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-1765%2891%2990221-6)). For GARCH(1,1) it is the ARCH(1) test, because the β score vanishes under the null, as Demos and Sentana restate. So ARCH-LM on SARIMAX residuals is a legitimate GARCH test that needs no GARCH fit. LM tests are not distorted by the boundary. One-sided versions that exploit non-negativity are more powerful, while size distortions grow and power falls with t(5) innovations ([Lee & King 1993](https://doi.org/10.1080/07350015.1993.10509930), with a [1994 correction](https://doi.org/10.2307/1391932); [Demos & Sentana 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-4076%2897%2900110-3)).

Power depends on the first autocorrelation of squared errors, α(1 − β² − αβ)/(1 − β² − 2αβ). That is **0.13 for the study's typical design and 0.21 for the persistent one** (recomputed here; the research notes' 0.19 and 0.34 are arithmetic slips). So detection is easier in the persistent design. The study's own power figures (LR 0.22-0.32 at T = 120, 0.46-0.78 at T = 250) show how little ten or twenty years of monthly data can say. Its ARCH-LM(4) false-positive rate of 3-7% fits the known picture of mild finite-sample distortion.

**The LR test is where textbooks mislead.** Under the null, α = 0 sits on the boundary and β is not identified. With one restricted parameter, **the LR follows a 50:50 mixture of χ²(0) and χ²(1), whose 5% critical value is 2.706 rather than 3.84**. Demos and Sentana tabulate such mixtures up to ARCH(12). With β free, the statistic is a supremum over β. Its distribution falls under Andrews' (2001) theory of boundary and nuisance parameters, whose worked examples include testing for conditional heteroskedasticity in a GARCH(1,1) regression ([Andrews 2001](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00210)). Hansen's (1996) simulated p-values and Andrews and Ploberger's (1994) optimal tests handle nuisance parameters that are unidentified under the null. Demos and Sentana note, however, that those procedures assume the null is away from the boundary ([Hansen 1996](https://doi.org/10.2307/2171789); [Andrews & Ploberger 1994](https://doi.org/10.2307/2951753)). The sources contain no tabulated critical value for the GARCH(1,1) sup-LR.

The study's rates fill that gap empirically (derived here). Because LR = sup over β of LR(β), it dominates the one-restriction mixture, so its null distribution lies above it. Every within-engine rate the study reports sits between that mixture and χ²(2), up to rounding:

| Rule | Cut-off on LR | Study's false-positive rate | 50:50 mixture | χ²(2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LR test | 5.99 | 1.5-5% | 0.7% | 5% |
| AIC | 4 | 6-12% | 2.3% | 13.5% |
| BIC | 9.6-13.8 (T = 120-1000) | 0-1% | below 0.1% | at most 0.8% |

The χ²(2) cut-off is therefore conservative and costs power, while the 2.706 cut-off would over-reject. The LM test, or a parametric bootstrap of the LR under the fitted homoskedastic null, avoids both problems.

Information criteria inherit the boundary. AIC and BIC assume regular parameter spaces ([Akaike 1974](https://doi.org/10.1109/TAC.1974.1100705); [Schwarz 1978](https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1176344136)). **Hughes, King and Kwek (2004)** derive a one-sided AIC whose penalties are probability-weighted: 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 for ARCH(1) to ARCH(4), against AIC's 1 to 4. In simulations at T = 60 and 200 they find AIC more liberal and BIC more conservative than their criterion ([Hughes, King & Kwek 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econlet.2003.05.003)). Their T = 60 case with an ARCH(1) truth (α 0.2) shows the cost of BIC's caution: BIC chose constant variance 67% of the time, AIC 48%.

Brooks and Burke found unmodified criteria unreliable for choosing conditional-variance orders. Their modified versions only sometimes improved forecasts ([Brooks & Burke 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/1351847021000029188)); earlier papers by the same authors point the same way, according to secondary summaries ([Brooks & Burke 1998](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0165-1765%2898%2900178-5); [Brooks & Burke 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9957.00323)). E6's false-positive rates of 7-12% for AIC and 0-1% for BIC fit that pattern. The specific rates are package-specific, and BIC's low false-positive rate comes with the low power Hughes et al. document.

**Fitting GARCH where there is none produces what boundary theory predicts.** Estimators with a true parameter on the boundary have non-normal limits ([Andrews 1999](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00082)). The GARCH QMLE with zero coefficients converges to a normal vector projected onto a convex cone ([Francq & Zakoian 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.spa.2007.01.001)). **Ma, Nelson and Startz (2007)** show that GARCH(1,1) satisfies the zero-information-limit condition. With α small, β's standard error shrinks too much even in very large samples and β̂ is biased upward. The t-test for β then has 44.5-48.7% size at α = 0.01 (T = 500-5000), and with true β = 0 the median β̂ is 0.32 ([Ma, Nelson & Startz 2007](https://doi.org/10.2202/1558-3708.1434)). Andrews and Cheng's identification-robust confidence sets are the general fix ([Andrews & Cheng 2012](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA9456)).

E6 finds α or β on a boundary in 64-87% of fits, which quantifies the pile-up; no source reports a frequency. The study's α = 0.08-0.10 designs sit in the moderately weak region of Ma, Nelson and Startz's tables, so the reported β̂ and its SE deserve a warning whenever α̂ is small. The small cost to the mean (RMSE within +4%, CRPS +0.0-0.3%) is what block-diagonality implies when the truth is a symmetric member of the GARCH family (an inference).

Ignoring GARCH when it is present costs more: inefficient estimates (Engle), anti-conservative AR/MA standard errors (weak ARMA), and bands that are right on average and wrong in turbulence (Christoffersen). Fitting an unneeded GARCH is cheap and omitting a needed one is not, provided the variance model's symmetry is correct, as E4 shows.

## Conclusion

The study's contribution is quantitative, plus one sign. Every mechanism it measures except E4's has been in print for decades, yet none of the sources contains a Monte Carlo of joint regression-ARMA-GARCH at monthly sample sizes, so its magnitudes — a 45-55% cross-backend AIC distortion, opg coverage of 0.83-0.92, a 64-87% boundary rate, a ten-point gap between calm and turbulent coverage — are new evidence for old theory. E4 is where the study runs ahead of the literature: theory allowed a misspecified variance to bias the mean, and the study supplies the sign, the size and the shifted-news-impact-curve mechanism. That changes the standard advice. "Add GARCH when the residuals show ARCH" is safe for intervals and for slopes, but for the intercept and the long-run mean it is safe only after an asymmetry check, because a symmetric GARCH fitted to leveraged data does more damage to the mean than SARIMAX does.

A second pattern runs through E1, E6 and the diagnostics: each misbehaving default compares against the wrong reference distribution, and the literature predicts the sign of each error. Cross-backend AIC is biased in a direction set by the units of y, the χ²(2) LR is conservative, `opg` is anti-conservative, and Jarque-Bera tests a null the t fit has already rejected; each has a cheap, literature-backed replacement that needs no new estimator, namely one-engine rescoring, an LM or one-sided LM pretest, sandwich standard errors, and Li-Mak and PIT checks. What the literature leaves open is narrower than the limitations list suggests. Tabulated sup-LR critical values for GARCH(1,1), the conditional-versus-exact gap near MA unit roots, and sandwich coverage under a misspecified variance have no published answers in the sources reviewed, and on all three the study's simulations are currently the best evidence available.
